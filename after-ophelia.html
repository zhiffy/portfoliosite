After Ophelia.

A two-part video project on identity, inherited interpretation, and the way a person can become what has been said about them.

We are all written by others, then rewritten by their machines.

View works → Back to works
Overview

The project follows Ophelia as an image that has been repeatedly narrated by others: first through Shakespeare, then through Millais, art history, online commentary, and AI summaries. It connects that history to the contemporary experience of being misdescribed online, where outdated or inaccurate records can become the material that machines treat as fact.

Rather than trying to recover a single true Ophelia, the work makes the construction visible. Each version of her becomes another layer in a larger system of image, explanation, repetition, and automation.

Process

Both works treat representation as a process that can erase as much as it preserves. Ophelia passes from voice to voice and medium to medium: character, painting, symbol, search result, commentary subject, and finally machine-readable summary.

For Ophelia, Retold, text fragments are gathered and spoken by an AI voice while a rendered figure searches through a space made from borrowed language. For Ophelia, Reassembled, 3D renders are transformed through AI image generation and left visibly unstable, so the breaks and artifacts become part of the work.

Artist statement

The project began from the experience of finding a personal image misrepresented online, then watching those descriptions harden into accepted references. Ophelia becomes the historical parallel: a figure almost entirely defined by interpretation, now recycled by algorithmic systems that flatten uneven sources into confident explanation.

In an age when AI can generate image and interpretation with the same ease, the work asks what happens when the line between representation and fabrication stops holding.

Supporting texts

Elaine Showalter - Representing Ophelia: Women, Madness, and the Responsibilities of Feminist Criticism (1985)

There is no "true" Ophelia for whom feminist criticism must unambiguously speak, but perhaps only a cubist Ophelia of multiple perspectives, more than the sum of all her parts.

The heroine of Hamlet has always been a blank screen on which generations of spectators have projected their own preoccupations. Each age constructs its own Ophelia, and each commentary becomes a mirror of its author's assumptions.

John Berger - Ways of Seeing (1972)

Men act and women appear. Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at.

This determines not only most relations between men and women but also the relation of women to themselves. The surveyor of woman in herself is male: the surveyed female. Thus she turns herself into an object, and most particularly an object of vision.

Roland Barthes - The Death of the Author (1967)

A text is made of multiple writings, drawn from many cultures and entering into mutual relations of dialogue, parody, and contestation.

There is one place where this multiplicity is focused: the reader, not the author. The birth of the reader must be at the cost of the death of the author.

Walter Benjamin - The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction (1936)

That which withers in the age of mechanical reproduction is the aura of the work of art.

What is jeopardized when authentic production is substituted by mechanical reproduction is the authority of the object.
